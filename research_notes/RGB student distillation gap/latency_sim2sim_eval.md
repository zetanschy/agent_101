# Latency/timing mismatch, sim-to-sim mismatch and evaluation protocol as inflators of the apparent teacher→student gap (and how to diagnose the source)

Context for the report writer: teacher (PPO, privileged state) scored 88/100 in mjlab (mujoco_warp, GPU, float32, dt 1/900, 30 substeps per 30 Hz action, 1.0-frame dead time at eval, 1–3 frames randomized in training). RGB student trained on data from the same mjlab env (fixed 1.0-frame dead time), with images re-rendered offline from saved qpos by CPU MuJoCo (textured, shadows), and scored 27/100 in a separate real-time CPU-MuJoCo "live twin" (30 Hz socket loop, 640x480 render → undistort → area-resize, ~12 ms median inference, its own servo-delay model). The teacher was never run in the twin. An earlier student: 57/100 in mjlab vs teacher 92/100; 0/20 in the twin when trained on flat untextured mjlab renders. The notes below are literature evidence plus clearly-marked inferences for this setup.

## Q1. Effect of observation/actuation latency on learned policies; delay randomization in training and in distillation; camera vs proprio latency mismatch

### Takeaway
Unmodelled or mis-modelled latency is one of the most frequently reported single causes of total sim-to-real (and by extension sim-to-sim) failure: policies trained with zero or a single fixed delay often oscillate or fail outright when the delay changes by even one or two control/sim steps, and plain delay randomization helps but does not fully fix it; giving the policy timing information or matching the delay distribution exactly does better. Vision pipelines add a second, larger and more variable delay than proprioception, so the image is stale relative to joint state, and papers that succeed model this asynchrony explicitly (separately for each modality).

### Cited Findings
- Tan et al. (RSS 2018, Minitaur) name "inaccurate actuator models and lack of latency modeling" as two major causes of the reality gap. Measured latency: 3 ms for the PD servo on the microcontroller, "typically 15–19 ms" for the locomotion controller on the Jetson TX2. They measured it by sending a one-step PWM spike and timing the resulting motor movement. — [Tan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10332)
- Tan et al. explain why missing latency breaks feedback policies: in simulation the policy gets instantaneous feedback, which "makes the stability region of a feedback controller in simulation much larger than its implementation on hardware"; sim-trained feedback policies "start to oscillate, diverge and ultimately fail in the real world." Their latency model keeps a timestamped observation history and linearly interpolates the observation at t − t_latency. — [Tan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10332)
- Tan et al. ablation: "both accurate actuator model and latency simulation are important. Without either of them, the learned controllers do not work on the real robot." Randomization ranges they used: latency 0–40 ms, control step 3–20 ms (because "the control step and latency can fluctuate because of the non real-time system"). — [Tan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10332)
- Hwangbo et al. (ANYmal, Science Robotics 2019): the actuator network takes joint position error and velocity at t, t−0.01 s and t−0.02 s; the history should be "sufficiently longer than the sum of all communication delays and the mechanical response time." With the ideal (zero-delay) or hand-tuned analytical actuator models, the policy "could not make a single step without falling" on hardware; torque prediction error was 0.740 Nm (actuator net) vs 3.55 Nm (ideal model) on validation and 0.966 vs 5.74 Nm on test data. The analytical model was tuned "for more than a week without much success." — [Hwangbo et al. 2019 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.08652)
- Sandha et al. (CoRL 2020, Time-in-State RL): policies degrade as sampling interval and execution latency vary, "even with state-of-the-art domain randomization techniques." Vanilla policies (trained at one fixed timing) in HalfCheetah/Ant "fail to work for execution latencies ≥ 8.24 ms, which is twice the SimTime" — i.e., a policy trained at a fixed delay broke when the delay merely doubled. On the real DeepRacer, both DR and TSRL policies worked at 20 ms execution latency, but at 60 ms and 100 ms "the performance of DR policies significantly degrade in comparison to the TS policies." TSRL adds the delay and sampling interval as observations; the TS policy learned to take slower actions as latency rose, whereas DR did not change its action speed. — [Sandha et al. 2020/2021, PMLR v155](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v155/sandha21a.html); code [nesl/Time-in-State-RL](https://github.com/nesl/Time-in-State-RL)
- Xiao et al. (ICLR 2020, "Thinking While Moving"): formalizes concurrent control where the next action is chosen while the previous one still executes; adding delay-aware features (previous action, "vector-to-go" = how much of the previous action is still outstanding, action-selection time) keeps Q-learning guarantees; on simulated and real vision-based grasping, concurrent models matched baseline success while acting 37% faster. — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06089); [project site](https://sites.google.com/view/thinkingwhilemoving)
- Imai et al. (MMDR, vision-guided A1 quadruped) measured real pipeline latencies: depth sensor 0.033 ± 0.004 s; network inference with vision 0.040 ± 0.007 s vs 0.004 s state-only; they report the latency between the depth sensor and proprioceptive sensor is "heavily asynchronous" and that this asynchrony "can cause large performance drop" in sim-to-real. Fix: randomize proprioceptive latency in [0, 0.04] s with linear interpolation, and for vision (30 Hz vs ~1 kHz proprio) keep a buffer of 4k depth frames and sample one frame from each k-frame chunk (separate per-modality delay models). — [Imai et al. 2021/2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.14549); [code](https://github.com/Mehooz/vision4leg)
- Agarwal et al. (egocentric-vision locomotion, CoRL 2022): the depth backbone output is sent over a UDP socket to the base policy with latency "10 ± 10 ms which we account for during phase 2" (i.e., during student distillation, not only teacher RL). They randomize height-map update period [80, 120] ms and update latency [10, 30] ms; an elevation-map baseline simulates a fixed 40 ms latency "in both the phases of training to match the hardware." Policy 50 Hz, PD 400 Hz. — [Agarwal et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07638)
- Lin et al. (vision-based dexterous humanoid manipulation, CoRL 2025) randomize delay stochastically: "frame lag probability 0.1" and "action lag probability 0.1", plus joint/action noise; policy and perception at 5 Hz. — [Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20396)
- Physical Intelligence's Real-Time Chunking (NeurIPS 2025): π0.5 model latency 76 ms (97 ms with RTC) on an RTX 4090, plus 10–20 ms for LAN remote inference; with injected +100/+200 ms delay, synchronous execution "degrades linearly" while RTC shows no degradation. In their Kinetix simulated benchmark (force-based control, no "holding position"), inference delay "necessitates asynchronous execution", and naive async degrades with delay d = 0…4 more than RTC. — [Black, Galliker, Levine 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07339)
- mjlab models actuation delay with "a wrapper class that buffers control signals and replays them with a latency quantized to the physics timestep"; observations can separately be "delayed by a configurable number of control steps." — [mjlab paper (Zakka et al. 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22074)
- Isaac Lab's `DelayedPDActuator` delays position/velocity/effort commands through a circular buffer; `min_delay`/`max_delay` are integers in physics steps, sampled uniformly at reset (default 0). — [Isaac Lab actuators docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/concepts/actuators.html)
- DextrAH-RGB (state teacher → stereo-RGB student via online DAgger in Isaac Lab, 60 Hz) does not describe any action/camera/observation delay modelling in teacher training or distillation (only sensor noise/bias). — [Singh et al. 2024, DextrAH-RGB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01791)
- Squint (SO-101, ManiSkill3, visual RL) also reports no action/observation delay randomization; policies were trained at 10 Hz and, for deployment, actions were scaled by 0.15 and the control rate tripled to 30 Hz — a deliberate train/deploy timing change that still transferred (73/80 real). — [Squint 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21203)
- A Hugging Face model card for an SO-101 ACT policy (imitation, not RL; community source, low authority) lists a measured 311 ms chunk-replan latency as one contributor to its sim-to-real gap and notes simulation hides it "because the sim waits for computation." — [omiya239532/so101_act_cotrain](https://huggingface.co/omiya239532/so101_act_cotrain)

### Inferences
- The student in this setup saw exactly one delay value (1.0 frame) in its whole dataset, whereas the teacher was robustified over 1–3 frames. By the Sandha et al. result (fixed-timing policies broke at 2× the training delay) and Tan et al. (oscillation/divergence of fixed-latency feedback policies), the student is the more delay-fragile of the two even before any distillation error. The literature practice (Agarwal 2022; Imai 2022; Lin 2025) is to randomize delay during the distillation/student phase as well, not only during teacher RL.
- In the live twin, the effective delay is: the twin's own servo-delay model + observation capture → render (640x480, shadows) → undistort/resize → socket → ~12 ms median inference (+ tail) → socket → actuation, all in wall-clock time. Unless the twin's servo model plus pipeline latency adds up to exactly what mjlab's 1.0-frame dead time represented, the student is evaluated off its training delay. At 30 Hz a frame is 33.3 ms, so the 12 ms median inference alone is ~0.36 of a frame and tail latencies can push some actions a whole frame late. (If "frame" in the user's setup means a 1/900 s physics frame rather than a 30 Hz control frame, the dead times are ~1.1–3.3 ms and the twin's pipeline latency would be an order of magnitude larger than anything seen in training; worth confirming which unit is meant.)
- Camera vs proprio staleness: if the twin reads joint state at one instant and renders the image at another (or the image goes through extra processing before reaching the policy), the image is stale relative to proprio by a variable amount. In mjlab-generated data both came from the same qpos snapshot, so the student never saw this skew. MMDR shows that per-modality asynchrony alone can cause large drops.
- Contact-rich grasping is the regime where timing errors matter most: a one-frame-late close/open command at a grasp moment changes contact outcomes, while free-space reaching is tolerant (Squint's reach tasks were 100% in sim and 10/10 real while stacking/grasping cans lost most).

### Gaps
- Could not verify whether RMA (Kumar et al. 2021) or "Learning to walk in minutes" (Rudin et al. 2021) explicitly randomize action/observation latency; not cited.
- No paper found that reports a controlled sweep of "student trained at a fixed delay vs student trained with randomized delay" for a distilled RGB manipulation student specifically; the closest evidence is locomotion (Agarwal, Imai) and generic control (Sandha).
- MMDR real-world quantitative comparison (MMDR vs No-Delay vs Frame-Extract) appeared only in a search snippet (992.5 vs 733.8 vs 572.4 average steps); I could not find those numbers in the downloaded PDF text, so they are omitted as unverified.
- No SO-100/SO-101/Koch RL sim-to-real report found that measures latency or randomizes delay; Squint (SO-101) does not mention it.

## Q2. Asynchronous (real-time) vs synchronous (lockstep) evaluation; real-time sim jitter

### Takeaway
Most simulation benchmarks are lockstep (the simulator waits for the policy), which hides inference latency and jitter; papers that care about timing either simulate a fixed delay d inside a lockstep loop (reproducible) or use real-time asynchronous execution (realistic but noisy). For diagnosis, the recommended pattern is to first reproduce the training timing exactly in lockstep, then add delay/jitter in controlled, logged amounts; frameworks expose explicit knobs for this. I found no paper that recommends scoring a policy only in an uncontrolled real-time loop.

### Cited Findings
- RTC authors: "Most simulated imitation learning benchmarks are quasi-static" and "standard simulation benchmarks are quasi-static—and have mostly been saturated with pseudo open-loop inference strategies"; they built a dynamic Kinetix benchmark where inference delay d (0–4 controller steps) is simulated explicitly. Synchronous execution produces pauses; naive asynchronous execution produces "jerky, out-of-distribution movements at chunk boundaries." — [Black et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07339)
- Xiao et al. frame standard RL as "blocking" (environment pauses while the agent computes) and show that concurrent (non-blocking) execution changes the MDP; information about elapsed/remaining action time must be in the state to recover correct value estimates. — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06089)
- Sandha et al. test policies "across a spread of different sampling intervals and execution latencies" and with jitter "between consecutive steps ... ±SimTime", and also with variable delays inside one episode (multi-tenancy settings) — an explicit protocol for timing robustness. — [Sandha et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v155/sandha21a.html)
- LeRobot's async inference splits a policy server from a robot client so the robot keeps acting while the next chunk is computed; exposed knobs include `--fps`, `--inference_latency` (target latency that throttles inference) and `--obs_queue_timeout`, plus a queue-size visualizer for tuning. — [LeRobot async docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/async)
- Real-is-Sim (RAI Institute, 2025): a dynamic digital twin synchronized with the real world at 60 Hz; the policy always acts on the simulated robot, and switching between real deployment and virtual evaluation is only toggling the real robot's follower; sim and real success rates on PushT correlated strongly. — [Abou-Chakra et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03597)
- Tan et al. randomize control step (3–20 ms) and latency (0–40 ms) because a non-real-time system fluctuates — i.e., they treat jitter as a domain-randomization variable rather than something to eliminate. — [Tan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10332)
- Isaac Lab recommends, when comparing backends, "compare a fixed reproduction at the intended policy period, then test timestep, substeps, and solver convergence separately." — [Isaac Lab solver differences](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/concepts/solver_differences.html)

### Inferences
- A real-time CPU-MuJoCo server stepping at dt 1/900 with 30 substeps per action plus a 640x480 textured, shadowed render per action can fall behind wall-clock time; whether it then slows down, skips, or lets the policy see older frames is an implementation detail that changes the effective delay per step. Logging per-step timestamps (obs capture, action receive, action apply, sim time) is the minimum needed to know what delay distribution the student actually experienced.
- The cleanest separation is: (1) run the twin in lockstep with a delay that exactly equals mjlab's 1.0-frame dead time; (2) then add the twin's servo model; (3) then real-time asynchrony. The score change at each step is the timing contribution. If the twin cannot do lockstep, a "pause physics until action arrives" mode is cheap to add and is what the benchmarks above effectively do.
- Real-time evaluation also makes runs non-reproducible, so 100-episode scores have extra variance; reporting a confidence interval (binomial, ±~9 points at p≈0.3, n=100) is needed before attributing a few-point difference.

### Gaps
- No found paper quantifies the success-rate difference between lockstep and real-time evaluation of the same manipulation policy in the same simulator; RTC's Kinetix results are reported as figures only (no numbers in text).
- No mjlab documentation found that discusses real-time or asynchronous evaluation.

## Q3. Sim-to-sim transfer (Isaac Gym/Isaac Lab → MuJoCo, MJX/mujoco_warp → CPU MuJoCo) as a sanity check; documented numerical/contact differences

### Takeaway
Sim-to-sim transfer to a second engine (usually CPU MuJoCo) is a standard pre-deployment check in legged locomotion, but it is mostly reported qualitatively; for contact-rich grasping the engines' friction/contact models differ enough that "equal coefficients do not guarantee equal slip, grasp stability, or contact counts." Even mujoco_warp vs CPU MuJoCo, which share the contact model, differ in precision (float32 vs float64), some colliders' contact counts, solver warm-start and options, and GPU determinism, and the MuJoCo team does not claim numerical parity.

### Cited Findings
- MuJoCo Warp docs: "MJWarp utilizes single precision float32 in contrast to MuJoCo's default double precision (float64)," and "Solver settings, including iterations, collision detection, and small friction values may be sensitive to differences in floating point representation." The FAQ answers "Why are numerical results from MJWarp and MuJoCo different?" with this precision difference. — [MJWarp docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mjwarp/)
- MJWarp collider differences: box-box uses GJK/EPA by default (up to 4 contacts) vs the `NATIVECCD`-flag primitive collider (up to 8); "Some MJWarp colliders have a different maximum number of contacts compared to MuJoCo" (e.g., PLANE<>MESH 4 vs 3); height-field contacts beyond mjMAXCONPAIR are ignored; exceeding `nconmax`/`njmax` causes "undefined behavior"; size/nconmax and size/njmax in the XML are not parsed. — [MJWarp docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mjwarp/)
- MJWarp solver differences: warmstart "always initializes the acceleration with qacc_warmstart" (MuJoCo picks between qacc_smooth and qacc_warmstart); tolerance clamped to ≥ 1e-6; `o_margin/o_solref/o_solimp/o_friction` overrides unavailable; different inertia factorization choices; PGS and noslip not supported. GPU results are not deterministic ("ordering or small numerical differences between results computed by different executions"). — [MJWarp docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mjwarp/); [mujoco_warp README](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp)
- MJX (JAX) differences: convex-mesh collisions use a branchless Separating Axis Test vs MuJoCo's MPR or GJK/EPA, meshes should have "roughly fewer than 32 vertices"; simpler broadphase; only CG and Newton solvers; users are told to reduce iterations/ls_iterations "just low enough that the simulation remains stable." — [MJX docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mjx.html)
- MuJoCo Playground discussion (beta): MuJoCo Warp has "good parity in terms of reward behavior" with MJX-JAX in Playground; JAX and Warp contact buffers differ, so contacts should be read through contact sensors. This is a JAX-vs-Warp, not Warp-vs-CPU, comparison. — [mujoco_playground discussion #197](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_playground/discussions/197)
- Isaac Lab (PhysX vs MJWarp): PhysX uses patch-based Coulomb friction, MJWarp uses MuJoCo's pyramidal/elliptic cone; "Equal material coefficients do not guarantee equal slip, grasp stability, or contact counts"; "More substeps change integration work; they do not translate a PhysX iteration setting into a Newton equivalent." Recommended: tune the target solver with its own controls after comparing physical behavior. — [Isaac Lab solver differences](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/concepts/solver_differences.html)
- mjlab explicitly lists "cross-simulator portability" as a non-goal and reports no quantitative sim-to-sim or sim-to-real numbers; it compiles MjModel on CPU and transfers it via put_model/put_data. — [mjlab paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22074)
- Humanoid-Gym trains in Isaac Gym and validates in MuJoCo (sim2sim) before zero-shot hardware deployment; MuJoCo was calibrated so leg-swing trajectories nearly match the real robot; no quantitative sim2sim success rates are given. — [Humanoid-Gym 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05695)
- Learning human-to-robot handovers from point clouds: a sim-to-sim test in a different physics simulator showed a performance loss, partly because the grasp predictor started grasping at the wrong time (a timing-sensitive failure). — [Christen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592)
- Collins et al. (real Kinova arm vs several simulators): simulators model manipulator kinematics and control accurately, but "the dynamic interactions of a simulation remain unsolved." — [Quantifying the Reality Gap in Robotic Manipulation Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01484)
- Lin et al.: real-to-sim autotune of physics/URDF constants with < 4 min of real data; lower tracking MSE correlated with transfer (best/median/worst MSE settings → 8/10, 3/10, 0/10 grasp success). — [Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20396)
- SIMPLER: perturbing mass, friction and joint friction changed success by "≤15%"; system-ID of PD gains reduced control loss from 0.267/0.432 to 0.131 and MMRV to 0.031. — [SIMPLER (Li et al. 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05941)

### Inferences
- The teacher's 88 and the student's 27 were measured in two different physics implementations (mujoco_warp float32 at dt 1/900 vs CPU MuJoCo float64 with whatever dt/solver/integrator/cone/impratio/margins the twin uses). For grasping, where small friction values and contact counts decide slip, this alone can produce a large drop, and the literature gives no reason to expect it to be small. The user's own earlier result (student 0/20 in the twin vs 57 in mjlab, rendering confounded) is consistent with a large environment gap.
- Replaying saved qpos into CPU MuJoCo for rendering is kinematic only and is not affected by the solver difference; the closed-loop rollout in the twin is.
- A cheap equivalence test: run the same open-loop action sequence (from a logged mjlab episode, including the dead-time model) in both engines and compare qpos trajectories and grasp outcome; divergence before the grasp points to actuator/delay modelling, divergence at contact points to contact/solver settings. This mirrors Isaac Lab's "fixed reproduction at the intended policy period" advice and Lin et al.'s calibration-trajectory approach.

### Gaps
- No published quantitative success-rate comparison found for the same grasping policy run in mujoco_warp/MJX vs CPU MuJoCo.
- No GitHub issue found documenting grasp-specific divergence between mujoco_warp and CPU MuJoCo.
- Integrator differences (Euler vs implicitfast) between the two engines are not documented beyond "IMPLICITFAST midpoint feature not supported" in MJWarp.

## Q4. Rendering domain gap between training renders and evaluation renders (resizing/antialiasing, undistortion, textures, lighting) and remedies

### Takeaway
CNN policies are sensitive to small, low-level pixel-statistics changes (few-pixel shifts, resize filters, texture, robot-arm appearance), and evaluation fidelity rises sharply only when the whole visual pipeline is matched jointly. Remedies with evidence: render in the loop with the same renderer and the exact same post-processing; render at higher resolution and area-downsample; visual domain randomization and color jitter; background/HDR randomization; pixel augmentation.

### Cited Findings
- Parmar et al. (CVPR 2022): "different image processing libraries produce drastically different results" for the same resize; PyTorch/TensorFlow use fixed-width bilinear filters independent of the downscale ratio (aliasing), PIL uses adaptive antialiased filters; JPEG compression also has a surprisingly large effect on the downstream metric. — [Parmar, Zhang, Zhu 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11222); [clean-fid](https://pypi.org/project/clean-fid/)
- Azulay & Weiss (JMLR 2019): modern CNNs "can drastically change their output when an image is translated by a few pixels," worse in deeper nets; the cause is aliasing from striding/pooling that ignores the sampling theorem; augmentation and antialiasing only partially fix it. — [Azulay & Weiss 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12177)
- SIMPLER visual-matching ablation (Google robot drawer tasks): real-vs-sim success gap 0.272 with no matching, 0.198 with only green-screen background, and 0.136 (MMRV 0.050) only with background + drawer + robot texture all matched — benefits appear "only if applied jointly and to all parts of a scene." Octo-Base scored 0% in sim with an untuned arm texture vs 29.3% with a tuned one. Patterned table textures cut success ~25% in real and ~24% in sim; solid colors ~4%/2%. Visual matching (MMRV 0.056, Pearson 0.924) beat variant aggregation (0.143, 0.778). — [SIMPLER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05941)
- Squint (SO-101): rendering at 128x128 and area-downsampling to 16x16 performs better than rendering directly at 16x16; removing color jitter cut real success from 91.3% to 72.5%; lowest resolutions trained in sim but did not transfer. — [Squint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21203)
- DextrAH-RGB distills with ray-traced tiled rendering in Isaac Lab, randomizes materials, dome-light HDRI (p=0.3, intensity 1000–4000), and applies random background (p=0.5), color jitter (p=1) and blur (p=0.1). — [DextrAH-RGB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01791)
- Lin et al.: camera pose noise, rotation noise 5°, FOV noise 5°; depth-only students failed (2/10, 0/10, 0/10, 0/10) whereas depth + 3D object position succeeded (10/10, 10/10, 9/10, 5/10) — i.e., an input-representation choice dominated transfer. — [Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20396)
- mjlab: "Photorealistic rendering is out of scope"; vision policies are expected to be distilled "using external rendering." — [mjlab paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22074)

### Inferences
- In this setup, training images came from CPU MuJoCo rendering of saved qpos, while evaluation images come from a 640x480 render → undistortion → area-resize pipeline in the twin. Unless the offline dataset rendering applied the identical undistort+resize code (same library, interpolation flag, dtype, color space, crop/black borders), the student sees a pixel-statistics shift. Given the Parmar and Azulay results, this can matter even when images "look the same." A byte-level test is cheap: render the same qpos through both pipelines and compare (max/mean absolute difference, and the student's action difference on the two images).
- Undistorting an image that MuJoCo rendered as an ideal pinhole image is not a no-op: it resamples (blurs) and may crop or add borders. If the twin's camera model includes distortion that the offline renderer did not, geometry also shifts slightly.
- The earlier 0/20 result with flat untextured mjlab renders is consistent with SIMPLER's finding that partial visual matching barely helps; the textured re-render fixed the gross gap, but residual post-processing differences are not ruled out.

### Gaps
- No paper found that quantifies the effect of camera undistortion resampling on a manipulation policy specifically.
- No quantitative study found of "CPU MuJoCo renderer vs mujoco_warp/Madrona batch renderer" pixel differences.

## Q5. Diagnostic protocols that separate distillation error from transfer (sim-to-sim, timing, rendering) error

### Takeaway
The literature's standard practice is to report the privileged teacher ("expert") and the student in the same environment under the same protocol, so the gap is pure imitation/observability error, and to treat validation (offline) action error as a poor proxy for closed-loop success. Querying the teacher on the student's own rollouts (DAgger-style, on-policy) measures where the student diverges; the irreducible "imitation gap" from privileged information can be separated by giving the student privileged inputs. No single paper lays out the full factorial protocol; the 2x2/3x2 matrix below is assembled from these practices.

### Cited Findings
- Hora (in-hand rotation, RMA-style teacher-student): results tables list the privileged "Expert" next to the student and baselines in the same simulator, and the student "closely emulat[es] the performance of the Expert"; the Expert is excluded from real-world tables "because we cannot access privileged information in the real world." Control 20 Hz, sim 120 Hz, PD 300 Hz. — [Qi et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.04887)
- DextrAH-RGB reports student success in simulation "normalized relative to the teacher's performance": 0.89 (fine-tuned ResNet, stereo, attention), 0.83 (mono), 0.71 (frozen ResNet). Real world: the student was below the depth-based DextrAH-G (per a summarizing read of Table II, ~80% stereo / 74.5% mono vs 92.7%; bin-packing 77/73% vs 87%), which the authors partly attribute to "sim2real transfer of dexterous manipulation policies is highly variable." KL loss to the teacher's action distribution "always out-performed" L2. — [DextrAH-RGB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01791)
- Squint: a State-to-Visual DAgger baseline (SAC state expert → visual student) reached 53/80 (66.3%) on the real SO-101 vs 73/80 (91.3%) for direct visual RL — evidence that distillation adds its own gap even with matched simulators. — [Squint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21203)
- Learning by Cheating: the privileged agent is a "white box" that "can be queried from any state of the environment, not only states that were visited," and provides "adaptive on-policy supervision" via online rollouts of the student — the basis for measuring action error on the student's own state distribution. — [Chen et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12294)
- DAgger: training on the learner's own state distribution addresses compounding error; behavior cloning's error can grow quadratically with horizon vs linearly for DAgger (well-known result; not re-read in this session). — [Ross, Gordon, Bagnell 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)
- ADVISOR: when the teacher acts on privileged information unavailable to the student, "this information is marginalized during imitation learning, resulting in an 'imitation gap'"; the gap's size "directly impacts agent performance." — [Weihs et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173)
- Scaffolder: privileged sensors used only in training-time components (critics, world models) let target policies "frequently perform comparably even to policies that have test-time access to the privileged sensors" — the "student with privileged inputs" upper bound is a standard comparison. — [Hu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14853)
- Copycat problem: imitation from observation histories can perform worse than from a single frame because the student learns to copy the previous expert action (recoverable from history) — relevant when the student's history encodes the delayed previous action. — [Wen et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14876)
- SIMPLER: "validation MSE is not a good proxy for a policy's real-world performance" (MMRV 0.375, Pearson 0.308 for validation MSE vs 0.056/0.924 for matched closed-loop simulation). — [SIMPLER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05941)
- Sandha et al. Appendix E: expose policies to variable delays within a single episode to quantify the drop as delay magnitude grows — a delay-sweep protocol. — [Sandha et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v155/sandha21a.html)
- Thinking While Moving: comparing models with/without delay-aware features is itself an ablation that isolates timing effects. — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06089)

### Inferences
Recommended diagnostic protocol for this setup (assembled from the practices above; each row/column changes one factor):
1. Teacher in the live twin (state observations, same servo model, same 30 Hz loop). If the teacher also drops far below 88, most of the 88→27 gap is environment/timing transfer, not distillation. This is the single most informative missing number.
2. Student in mjlab closed loop with images rendered in the loop by the same CPU-MuJoCo renderer + identical undistort/resize pipeline as the twin (render from the current mjlab qpos each step). Student(mjlab) vs teacher(mjlab) = distillation + observability gap; student(mjlab) vs student(twin) = transfer gap. The earlier 57 vs 92 suggests the in-sim distillation gap alone may be ~35 points.
3. Timing ladder in the twin: lockstep with exactly 1.0-frame dead time → add the twin's servo model → add real inference latency in lockstep (simulated d) → full real-time asynchronous. Run teacher and student at each rung. A delay sweep (0, 1, 2, 3 frames) for both shows whether the student is more delay-fragile than the teacher (expected, since its data had one delay value).
4. Physics ladder: same logged open-loop actions in both engines; then the teacher closed-loop in CPU MuJoCo with mjlab-matched dt/substeps/solver/cone/impratio/float settings vs the twin's settings.
5. Rendering check: for logged twin states, compare the student's action on the twin image vs on the offline-pipeline image of the same qpos (action delta), and pixel-level differences.
6. Oracle ablations: replace one student input at a time with the privileged/teacher quantity (e.g., object pose, or the teacher's own action at grasp time) to see which missing information accounts for the gap (imitation-gap measurement, ADVISOR/Scaffolder style).
7. On-policy action error: on the student's own twin and mjlab rollouts, query the teacher at every step (teacher is a white box with state available in sim) and plot ||a_student − a_teacher|| vs time, aligned to grasp/lift events. Combined with a histogram of when failures occur (time of first irrecoverable event: missed grasp, slip, drop), this shows whether errors start at a specific phase (e.g., gripper close timing, which is delay-sensitive) or accumulate (compounding error).
8. Report each score with a binomial confidence interval; 100 episodes gives about ±9 points at p≈0.3.

### Gaps
- No single published paper found that runs the full teacher/student × train-env/eval-env matrix for an RGB manipulation student; the protocol above is an inference from separate practices.
- No paper found that plots failure-onset time along the episode as a standard teacher-student diagnostic; this is a recommendation, not a cited practice.
- Hora student numbers and DextrAH-RGB teacher absolute success in sim were not extracted (only normalized student scores and the Expert row's existence).
